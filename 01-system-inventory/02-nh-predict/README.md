# System 2: nH Predict — AI Governance Case Study

This inventory documents nH Predict’s technical function and the workflow it operates within. NIST AI RMF, the EU AI Act, and ISO 42001 all define risk, classification, and oversight in terms of deployment context and organizational process — not the model in isolation.

This artifact examines nH Predict based on public reporting, government oversight findings, and allegations in pending litigation. Lokken v. UnitedHealth Group is an active case. The allegations described here are unproven claims, not established fact. UnitedHealth disputes them.
## Purpose
nH Predict predicts length of stay for Medicare Advantage patients in skilled nursing facilities. naviHealth built it. UnitedHealth owns naviHealth, and renamed it Home & Community Care in 2024. UnitedHealth calls nH Predict a care-planning tool, shared with providers and caregivers. A class action, Lokken v. UnitedHealth Group, disputes that. The prediction is generated near the start of the stay. It then governs coverage for the rest of the stay: care past the predicted date requires documented justification to override it.

The lawsuit makes two separate claims. First, the prediction itself is inaccurate — plaintiffs cite a 90% reversal rate on appealed denials. Second, naviHealth enforced the prediction rigidly — case managers who deviated from it, even for justified additional care, faced discipline or termination.

This artifact covers only that function: the length-of-stay prediction governing ongoing coverage after admission. A separate prior authorization gate controls admission itself. naviHealth administers that too, but it’s not part of this artifact.

## Jurisdiction
nH Predict applies to Medicare Advantage enrollees receiving skilled nursing facility care, across all U.S. states. Enrollees qualify for Medicare either by age, 65 and older, or by disability — SSDI, ALS, or End-Stage Renal Disease. Jurisdiction follows the enrollee, not the company. Where UnitedHealth or naviHealth are incorporated doesn’t matter. Where the enrollee lives does. Medicare Advantage is a federal program, regulated nationally by CMS, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. Some aspects of plan administration still vary by state — network adequacy, certain consumer protections.

## Risk Tier
High. nH Predict’s predictions affect Medicare Advantage patients’ access to skilled nursing facility care, with direct financial and health consequences if coverage ends before recovery is complete. CMS does not publish a formal risk-tier framework the way the EU AI Act or FDA do. CMS’s 2024 guidance instead sets a compliance standard: coverage determinations must rely on the individual patient’s circumstances, not a population-level algorithm alone. That standard is the backdrop against which this system’s risk should be judged.

CMS has named this specific risk. Its guidance warns that algorithms can exacerbate discrimination and bias, and that MAOs must ensure their use doesn’t violate Section 1557 nondiscrimination protections. There’s already a documented disparity that raises the question directly: HHS’s Office of Inspector General found MAOs and their contractors denied SNF-level care requests from existing nursing home residents 40% of the time, compared to an 11% denial rate for all other enrollees — nearly four times higher. Public reporting doesn’t break that figure down by naviHealth specifically, or by race, sex, or other protected characteristics. But a nearly fourfold disparity by patient population, in the exact use case CMS flagged for discrimination risk, is not a finding to set aside.

## Model Type
naviHealth hasn’t published nH Predict’s architecture. What’s documented is behavior, not technique. The system compares a patient’s data — diagnosis, age, living situation, physical function — against a database of roughly six million historical cases. It produces a predicted length of stay and target discharge date based on similar prior cases. That behavior fits a case-based or nearest-neighbor comparison approach. It could also be a regression model trained on the same historical data. Public reporting doesn’t say which. The technique is proprietary.

## Human Oversight
naviHealth case managers enter patient data. They get back nH Predict’s projected length of stay. The prediction is generated once, near the start of the stay, and it governs coverage for what follows. Coverage past that date needs documented justification to override it. Public reporting doesn’t describe a mechanism for the prediction to update from new clinical data during the stay. Case managers document exceptions. The model doesn’t appear to revise its forecast in response to them.

And naviHealth’s architecture is undisclosed. Oversight depends on a reviewer understanding why a model produced a given output. When the architecture is proprietary, that understanding isn’t available. Case managers are reviewing a number with no visibility into how it was generated.

Plaintiffs allege naviHealth enforced the prediction rigidly. Case managers who deviated from it were disciplined or terminated, regardless of whether the additional care was justified.

“Human in the loop” is a presence claim, not a function claim. A case manager sees the prediction. They can, in principle, act on it. What the presence of a reviewer doesn’t establish is whether that reviewer can reach a different conclusion without personal cost. If deviating from a system’s output carries professional risk, review stops functioning as review. It becomes compliance with the system, dressed as oversight of it. CMS requires more than presence. Its guidance states that coverage determinations must account for the individual patient’s circumstances, not a population-level prediction alone. A case manager penalized for departing from that prediction isn’t applying individual judgment. They’re applying the algorithm’s judgment, under their own name.

The risk here doesn’t fall evenly. The case manager absorbs professional risk for exercising independent judgment — discipline, termination. CMS’s enforcement authority, civil monetary penalties and enrollment suspension, falls on UnitedHealthcare as the Medicare Advantage sponsor, not on the case manager and not on naviHealth as a subsidiary. The individual bears the cost of disagreeing with the system. The organization bears the cost only if a pattern gets caught.

## Data Sources
Provenance. nH Predict draws on a database naviHealth compiled from its own work with providers. Collection started in the mid-1990s, decades before UnitedHealth acquired naviHealth in 2020. This isn’t CMS claims data. UnitedHealth didn’t build it either. It’s a proprietary asset naviHealth accumulated over the years, and UnitedHealth acquired it along with the company. Public sources don’t even agree on its size — 3 million records in one place, 4 million in another, 6 million in coverage of the litigation. That inconsistency raises a real question about version control, one public documentation doesn’t answer.

Scope mismatch. The database isn’t specific to skilled nursing facilities. naviHealth manages post-acute care across settings — skilled nursing, inpatient rehabilitation, long-term acute care, and home health — and the historical case database spans all of them. This artifact’s scope is skilled nursing facility care. It’s not clear from public reporting whether a SNF patient’s prediction draws on a comparison population limited to other SNF patients, or on the full mixed-setting database. That distinction matters. A model comparing a SNF patient to a database that includes home health and inpatient rehab cases is comparing across settings with different staffing, different acuity, and different expected recovery paths.

Validation gap. Nothing is publicly documented about how this data gets cleansed, normalized, updated, or retained. There’s no disclosed process for adding new cases, correcting errors, or purging old records. There’s no published validation methodology. There’s no disclosed cadence for testing the model’s ongoing accuracy. Plaintiffs cite a 90% reversal rate on appealed denials. But that figure measures downstream decision outcomes, and only among the small share of patients who appeal — about 11.5% of denials, by one government estimate. It doesn’t speak to the accuracy or integrity of the underlying training data. No public source evaluates the data itself.

Interaction-effects gap. It’s also unclear whether the comparison accounts for how these variables interact, or treats them separately. A patient’s diagnosis, age, and physical function don’t act independently — a comorbidity can change a patient’s recovery path in ways a single variable can’t capture on its own. Critics have raised this directly: the algorithm reportedly doesn’t account for comorbidities or complications that arise during the stay, which can produce estimates that are too restrictive for a specific patient’s actual course. Whether the underlying model captures those interactions, or matches on variables individually and averages across similar cases, isn’t publicly documented.

Access denial. When patients or physicians have asked to see nH Predict’s underlying reports, UnitedHealth has denied those requests. The information is proprietary, they say.

## Closing
Six fields, and most of them end the same way: undocumented. Not because the law requires it. HIPAA protects a patient’s individual health record — it says nothing about whether a company discloses how its model works, how it’s validated, or where its training data comes from. naviHealth withholds that on other grounds. UnitedHealth’s own word for it, per the lawsuit, is proprietary. A patient whose coverage depends on this system can’t see the data it was trained on, can’t see how the model works, and can’t confirm whether anyone checks its accuracy. None of that would expose any patient’s identity. What that patient can see is the bill. Opacity has a direction here. The system’s workings stay private, by choice, not by law. The consequences stay public.
